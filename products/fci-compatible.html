FCI Cable Assemblies: Selection to Production

An FCI harness is not one standard cable. It may use discrete crimped wires, an IDC ribbon, a permanent board termination or a power connector, depending on the equipment. This guide follows the decisions that turn those parts into a defined assembly: selecting the interface, understanding materials, managing installation risks and controlling the production version. Series names are engineering references; component ratings and brand names do not automatically qualify the finished harness.

01

FCI Connector Harness Features

FCI Basics is part of the Amphenol connector portfolio. A custom harness built around these interfaces may join a control board, display, power module or removable subassembly. The useful features are specific: circuit density, mating orientation, contact retention and a termination method suited to the cable. No single FCI label guarantees sealing or high-speed performance. Super Smile defines the assembly from an approved connector set, netlist, dimensions and inspection requirements.

02

Minitek: Match the Pitch and Termination

Minitek 2.00mm includes modular wire-to-board, flat-cable and board connections. The separate 1.00mm wire-to-board system serves finer wiring and has its own contacts and headers. Keep the complete series and part number on the drawing: a shared product name does not make different pitches interchangeable. Check row count, entry direction, wire range and latch access before selecting the housing. Cable routing should be reviewed with the actual mating board in place.

03

Dubox 2.54mm Crimp-to-Wire

Dubox includes crimp-to-wire as well as board-mounted variants. Its dual-beam contact arrangement and available orientations support different equipment layouts, but the cable housing must match the specified header and contact. Do not order a generic 2.54mm plug by appearance. Retain the plating suffix and dimensional drawing, and distinguish catalogue current per contact from a fully loaded multi-circuit assembly under its actual temperature conditions.

04

Quickie IDC and Flat Ribbon Cables

Quickie uses 2.54mm-pitch IDC technology for cable-to-board connections. IDC termination displaces the insulation to contact the conductor, so cable geometry and the intended termination tooling are central to the process. Define conductor spacing, wire construction, pin-one orientation and any foldback under the strain-relief cover. A connector pitch is not necessarily the spacing between adjacent ribbon conductors. Verify the complete connector and cable drawing rather than assuming they are the same.

05

Board-In and Blind-Mate Are Different

Minitek Board-In is a one-piece direct solder-to-board solution for permanent wiring. A blind-mate interface instead helps removable parts connect where the mating point cannot be seen easily. For a blind-mate project, budget the enclosure, board and connector positional tolerances together; floating travel does not absorb unlimited misalignment. For Board-In, review solder processing and mechanical support so that cable movement does not load the soldered termination.

06

Minitek Pwr: Power Requires Derating

Minitek Pwr covers unsealed wire-to-wire and wire-to-board connections in several pitch families. Positive locks and isolated contacts address handling and mating, not waterproofing. Select conductor size with the contact range, circuit loading, ambient temperature and permitted temperature rise in mind. A thicker wire is not useful if it cannot be terminated correctly. Include the complete supply-and-return path when reviewing voltage drop and protective-device coordination.

07

Terminal Contact Material

The spring section, mating surface and crimp barrel perform different jobs. Copper-alloy contacts, including phosphor-bronze examples in FCI documentation, are chosen with geometry and finish as a system. Do not replace a terminal using only its outline or color. Confirm the approved wire construction and insulation support, then define inspection points for strand capture, barrel formation and contact damage. Material identity alone does not establish a good crimp.

08

Insulator Base and Housing Polymers

Connector housings may use different engineering resins depending on the part. Minitek 1.00mm documentation, for example, identifies PA9T, while Dubox variants include glass-filled thermoplastic polyester. LCP, PBT and PA66-GF should not be described as one universal blend. Review temperature exposure, soldering process, chemical contact and latch behavior against the selected grade. A component material flammability classification does not certify the complete harness or equipment.

09

Tolerance Control and Circuit Density

Closer contacts make assembly accuracy more important, but a smaller pitch does not automatically improve every electrical characteristic. Check housing dimensions, board position and enclosure constraints using the referenced drawings. Inspect insertion orientation and damaged pins before attempting to mate. A tolerance stack can prevent a correct connector pair from seating once installed; solve the mounting geometry rather than using cable force to pull the two halves together.

10

NBR, FKM and Sealed Variants

NBR and FKM are elastomer families with different fluid and temperature behavior; neither is a universal answer to every chemical exposure. More importantly, many FCI Basics interfaces are unsealed. A waterproof MicroSpaceXS version must be distinguished from an unsealed version and assembled with its specified sealing parts. Confirm the compound, wire outside diameter, unused cavities and mating state before agreeing an ingress-protection requirement.

11

High-Speed Signal Integrity

The complete channel includes cable geometry, connector transitions, return paths and board routing. Fine pitch alone does not guarantee bandwidth, and not every FCI signal connector contains an effective cable shield. Start with the interface requirements and permitted length. Review pair assignment, untwisted sections and screen termination, then agree the appropriate measurements. Continuity is necessary for correct wiring but cannot replace an insertion-loss, crosstalk or eye-diagram evaluation where required.

12

Polarization and Locking

A keyed housing helps guide orientation, while a latch retains the mated connector and a contact-retention feature holds the terminal in its cavity. These are different functions. Verify full engagement and access for release; never force a mismatched connector into place. Where multiple identical plugs sit together, keys alone may not prevent a wrong circuit connection. Use deliberate coding, cable identification and a clear installation drawing.

13

PVC and Fluoropolymer Wire Insulation

PVC and fluoropolymer-insulated wires address different combinations of cost, dimensions and exposure. PTFE, FEP and other fluoropolymers are not interchangeable specifications. Select an exact wire construction and confirm whether the terminal accepts its conductor and insulation dimensions. Thin insulation can change handling and stripping requirements. Temperature or chemical resistance claimed for the wire does not automatically apply to the connector, label, sleeve or complete assembly.

14

Plastic Circular Housings: Check the Series

Round geometry is a packaging choice, not proof of sealing, torsional strength or interchangeability. If a project specifies a circular connector alongside an FCI board interface, identify both ends separately and record the coupling system, pin layout and panel mounting. Check the cable gland or rear support against the jacket diameter. A transition harness can combine different connector types, but each interface retains its own limits.

15

Gold Flash and Contact Finish

Gold flash describes a thin finish, not a universal lifetime or low-resistance guarantee. Thickness, porosity, underplating and the mating contact influence performance. FCI pre-crimped lead options distinguish terminal and finish suffixes; keep those details in the BOM. Do not infer plating from color or mix contact options without review. Cleaning and rework should preserve the intended mating surface rather than polish away its protective layer.

16

Oil and Water Exposure

Describe the fluid, concentration, temperature and exposure pattern: occasional splash, continuous immersion and pressure washing are not the same requirement. Assess the jacket, seals, labels and branching protection together. Fluid can travel along an unsealed cable end even when the jacket surface resists it. A practical design review therefore considers the complete entry path and installation, followed by an agreed material or assembly test where needed.

17

Automotive Applications

Protected cabin wiring, lighting modules and exposed equipment have different connector needs. Begin with installation location, load profile and the exact mating module. Distinguish low-voltage controls from high-voltage propulsion circuits and check vehicle-specific identification before building a service harness. Automotive marketing does not replace the required drawing, test scope or approval. Samples should be evaluated in the intended mounting position, including the access needed to release the connector.

18

Industrial Automation

PLC cabinets, machine controls and sensor modules benefit from planned branch positions and readable circuit identification. Keep motor power, switching loads and sensitive signals organized according to the system design. A fixed board connector does not qualify the cable for drag-chain motion. Define moving and stationary sections, clamp positions and maintenance access before selecting the harness protection. Serviceability is part of the design, not an extra length added at the end.

19

Telecommunications and Data Equipment

Network equipment can contain internal power leads, fan wiring, management signals and high-speed links in the same enclosure. Classify them before choosing connectors. An ordinary discrete-wire harness must not be described as a fiber-optic or high-speed channel merely because the supplier offers those products elsewhere. Review cooling, module removal and cable bend paths together. The requested data interface determines the cable construction and verification, not the general FCI brand name.

20

Medical Devices

Internal connections in monitors, analyzers and imaging equipment need documented electrical functions and mechanical routing. The intended use determines separation, cleaning and validation requirements. Do not treat a component rating as approval for patient contact, sterilization or a complete medical device. Supply the applicable equipment requirements during review so that material selection, labeling and inspection records support the actual project rather than a generic medical claim.

21

Consumer Electronics

In a compact appliance or embedded controller, a few millimeters of connector height can change the enclosure layout. Confirm cable exit, cover clearance and assembly order before finalizing length. Check that the harness cannot be pinched by a screw boss or pulled across a sharp edge. Where the product is serviced, preserve a clear release path and identification at both ends; assembly speed should not depend on guessing plug orientation.

22

Aerospace and Defence Review

These projects may require controlled sourcing, process approval and environmental evidence beyond a catalogue connector specification. State the equipment function, traceability and acceptance requirements before selection. We separate an initial feasibility review from an approved production commitment. No general FCI family name proves military qualification or flight suitability, and a reference photograph of aerospace equipment is not evidence of supplying that equipment.

23

Loose Pins and Incomplete Seating

An intermittent connection may come from a terminal that never seated, a damaged retention feature or cable load transferred into the cavity. Check the specified terminal and housing combination before blaming the contact material. Inspect seating and retention using the approved method, and confirm any secondary lock. Repeatedly pushing a damaged contact back into place is not a controlled repair; define replacement and reinspection requirements.

24

Corrosion and Contamination

Moisture, salts and process residues can affect exposed mating surfaces. A plated contact does not eliminate the need to control exposure, packaging and handling. Inspect where contaminants can enter and whether the connector is actually sealed in its installed state. Avoid unapproved cleaners or abrasives on the contacts. A failure investigation should record the location and operating history before replacing parts, so that the cause is not lost.

25

Wire Fatigue at the Transition

Repeated bending often concentrates where a flexible cable meets a rigid connector, sleeve or clamp. Review the transition radius and motion path rather than simply adding more tape. Choose a cable construction intended for the duty cycle and keep the moving zone away from unsupported terminations. Strand count alone does not establish flex life. Any life claim needs a defined test path, load, speed and failure criterion.

26

Poor Shielding and Uncontrolled Bonds

Noise problems can remain even with a screened cable if the screen ends in a long, poorly controlled connection. Identify the interference source and intended return path before changing the shield. Keep termination geometry and chassis bonding consistent with the equipment design. Adding a second braid or a ferrite at random can complicate assembly without solving the cause. Compare the revised installation against an agreed measurement, not only subjective improvement.

27

Housing Cracks and Mechanical Loads

A cracked housing can indicate impact, incorrect insertion, chemical exposure or an installation load the part was not designed to carry. Check the mounting stack, latch clearance and cable restraint before proposing a different resin. The correct response may be a routing change or support feature rather than a harder material. Damaged housings should be assessed under a defined rework rule, not hidden beneath a sleeve or overmould.

28

Wrong Insertion and Look-Alike Plugs

Several identical connectors can fit physically while carrying different circuits. Prevent errors through cavity mapping, labels and deliberate routing, with keyed variants where the system permits. Confirm pin numbering from the specified mating or wire-entry view because those views can appear mirrored. An assembly drawing should show the reference view explicitly. Final testing then checks the approved netlist rather than a cable that merely resembles the sample.

29

Durability Under Real Conditions

Vibration, thermal cycling, ingress and mating wear are separate exposures with different test methods. Define which are relevant to the finished assembly, how it is mounted and which circuits are monitored. A latch or strain-relief feature does not alone prove survival under every condition. Record specimen configuration and acceptance criteria so results can be compared between revisions. Catalogue component data should remain distinguishable from project-specific harness evidence.

30

Custom Length and Wire Gauge

Define length datums at the housing face, cable exit or another agreed drawing feature. Include tolerances and branch positions, not only an overall length. Wire size depends on current, voltage drop, temperature and the terminal application range. A larger conductor can increase stiffness and reduce routing clearance, so electrical and mechanical choices must be reviewed together. The sample should demonstrate fit in the intended equipment before production dimensions are frozen.

31

Custom Connector Selection

Provide full part numbers for both cable ends and the equipment-side mates. Decide whether original Amphenol/FCI parts are required or whether a separately evaluated alternative may be proposed. Preserve plating, keying, wire range and retention options in the BOM. A new supplier or revised part suffix should trigger review rather than automatic substitution. Sample approval should cover the actual mating set, not an isolated loose connector.

32

Overmoulding and Strain Relief

An overmould can organize the cable exit and provide a designed transition, but it must not obstruct the latch or distort the connector. Review resin compatibility, processing exposure, wall thickness and cable support with the selected parts. Do not assume that covering the rear of an unsealed connector makes it waterproof. Tooling and first-article inspection must confirm dimensions, mating access and any project-defined environmental performance.

33

Shielding Options and EMI/RFI Suppression

Choose foil, braid, drain-wire or ferrite arrangements to address an identified coupling path. Ferrite material and impedance vary with frequency, and placement relative to the noise source matters. Document the component, cable turns and position so the tested arrangement can be reproduced. Filtering is not a substitute for correct pinout, grounding and routing. Any EMC improvement claim should be supported by a repeatable equipment-level comparison.

34

From Sample to Controlled Supply

A useful enquiry includes drawings or a sample, verified pinout, mating references, quantities and acceptance requirements. We review open points before defining materials and a sample build. Production release should align the approved sample, drawing revision, BOM and inspection plan. Record changes and lot identification where required, and protect connector faces during packing. This creates a repeatable supply definition instead of relying on an informal photograph of the first piece.

Engineering and Purchasing Questions

Are Amphenol and FCI related?

Amphenol acquired FCI in 2016. FCI Basics is now within its connector portfolio. The exact series and complete part number still determine the interface.

Does compatible guarantee original FCI parts?

No. The approved BOM must state original parts or a separately reviewed alternative. Neither appearance nor a broad compatibility label is sufficient.

Are all FCI connectors waterproof?

No. Many board and power interfaces are unsealed. Select the explicit sealed version, mating parts and cable-entry components before discussing assembly protection.

How should I read a cable pinout?

First identify the connector view and pin-one reference. Then map each cavity to its signal and opposite end. Wire color supports identification but does not replace the netlist.

Can length, gauge and connector combinations be customized?

Yes, after checking the mating interface, terminal wire range and installation. Custom wiring does not change the connector’s manufactured mating geometry.

What determines the price?

Connector sourcing, wire and finish choices, circuit count, length, protection, tooling, inspection and order quantity all contribute. Quote against a defined revision rather than an incomplete description.

Which tests should be specified?

Start with the approved circuit map and dimensional checks, then identify additional electrical or environmental requirements. Methods, limits and sample scope must be agreed for the actual assembly.
