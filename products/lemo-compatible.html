Push-Pull Connections, Engineered as a Complete Cable

This category includes instrument leads, extensions, adapters, coiled cables and equipment harnesses, not one fixed product. The following guide moves from connector selection to use conditions, surface treatment and strain relief, then to sample approval. It separates the connector specification from the behavior of the finished cable so the requirements remain clear when a project moves into production.

01

LEMO Interfaces: Define the Complete Assembly

A push-pull cable can connect a sensor, instrument, camera or removable machine module. The interface is only one part of the design: cable construction, circuit assignment, shielding, length and support points determine how the complete assembly behaves. Super Smile can review straight leads, extensions, branches and coiled configurations against supplied drawings or mating samples. Original-brand components and approved alternatives must be identified separately in the bill of materials.

02

Push to Latch, Pull the Sleeve to Release

A self-latching push-pull mechanism retains the plug after insertion; normal release uses the outer sleeve rather than pulling the cable. Installation space must therefore include room for fingers or the specified handling tool. Check full engagement with the actual receptacle, not only an audible click. Key alignment and latch engagement are different checks, and a cable restraint should prevent routine loads from being carried by the connector.

03

B Series for Protected Equipment Connections

The B series uses keyed metal push-pull interfaces for protected environments. Its standard IP50 designation must not be presented as waterproofing. Select shell size, contact arrangement, termination and cable collet together; different versions within a family can have different needs. A panel receptacle also needs suitable mounting thickness and access behind the panel. Review the wiring path with neighboring connectors fitted so that the release sleeve remains usable.

04

K and T: Sealing Is an Assembly Condition

Outdoor keyed families such as K and T provide specified sealed configurations, but the rating applies under defined mating and assembly conditions. Cable roundness, jacket diameter, seal selection and the rear termination all matter. A sealed front interface cannot stop moisture entering an unsealed cable branch elsewhere. Drawings should state the exposure, mated or unmated condition and any cap requirement before an ingress verification method is agreed.

05

S and E: Stepped Inserts and Cable Groups

S and E families use stepped-insert configurations, including unipole and multipole options. S generally serves protected environments, while E includes watertight configurations with the appropriate mating and cable assembly. Do not choose a mate from outer diameter or pin count alone. Coaxial contacts additionally require the correct cable group; a cable with a familiar RG designation still needs the contact and assembly dimensions checked.

06

REDEL Plastic Interfaces for Handling and Identification

REDEL P, SP and MP are distinct connector systems rather than interchangeable plastic versions of one metal plug. Shell geometry, keying, color coding and contact arrangement influence handling and identification. The selected material must also suit the equipment cleaning routine. In a medical project, connector selection remains separate from device-level electrical safety, biocompatibility or validated sterilization; those requirements must come from the customer specification.

07

Industrial and Robotic Cable Routing

A moving connection is a system of cable, connector, clamps and available travel. Define repeated bending separately from torsion, then select a cable intended for that duty. Keep the flexing zone away from solder joints and rigid backshell transitions. Check minimum bend radius under the actual load and movement, not only on a static bench sample. Functional monitoring during an agreed motion test can reveal intermittent faults that a stationary continuity test misses.

08

Coiled Leads: Reach, Return and Pull Force

A coiled lead provides changing reach, but its restoring force still acts on the equipment. Specify relaxed length, usable extended length, coil diameter and straight tails separately. Review the working position at both travel limits and provide a support point where necessary. Excessive extension can permanently distort the coil or load the connector. The electrical path follows the entire conductor length, so voltage drop and signal behavior cannot be estimated from the relaxed coil length.

09

Medical Electronics: Start with the Use Environment

A handheld diagnostic probe, a patient-adjacent lead and an internal instrument cable have different exposure and safety requirements. Describe cleaning agents, contact duration, reuse policy and handling cycles before selecting jacket and connector materials. Color and keying can help users identify channels, but cannot replace electrical safeguards. Any insulation, leakage-current, cleaning or environmental verification must be defined for the finished device and its approved accessories.

10

Contact Plating and Shell Finish Are Different

Electrical contacts and external shells perform different jobs. A contact finish supports the mating surface, while a shell finish also responds to handling and environmental exposure. Record the actual material and finish code instead of describing the whole connector as gold plated. Plating thickness, porosity, contact force and the mating finish interact; color is not an acceptance measurement. Replacement contacts must retain the approved finish and dimensional specification.

11

Read Surface-Treatment Standards by Their Scope

A standard number is useful only when attached to the correct material, process and acceptance clause. LEMO catalog references distinguish chrome treatment under SAE AMS 2460, nickel under AMS QQ-N-290 or a stated alternative, and gold under ISO 27874. These are not interchangeable labels. If a project requires documented plating, specify the exact component, revision and evidence required; a catalog citation is not certification of our complete cable assembly.

12

Salt, Moisture and Cleaning Residues

Corrosion evaluation begins with where liquid can collect and how dissimilar materials meet. A laboratory salt-spray duration describes one test procedure, not an equivalent number of years outdoors. Examine contacts, shell joints, fasteners and cable exits separately. Cleaning residues or damaged finishes can change behavior after assembly. Agree the exposure method and acceptance criteria for the intended application instead of applying one advertised hour count to every configuration.

13

Mating Cycles Are Not Cable Flex Life

Connector mating endurance and cable bending endurance are different measurements. A published component cycle rating uses specified conditions; it does not cover pulling the lead, contamination, side loading or an unsuitable cable exit. Define how often the connector is handled and how the cable moves in service. For frequently changed probes, inspect latch action, contact condition and the strain-relief transition together rather than counting insertions alone.

14

Test and Measurement: Protect the Signal Path

A sensor extension can change the measurement chain through resistance, capacitance, shielding and additional contacts. Record whether the signal is low-level analog, a thermocouple, a coaxial channel or a digital interface. Cable selection and pin allocation follow that signal, not the appearance of the plug. For low-level measurements, grounding and cable movement may be as important as continuity. Compare the assembled lead in the actual instrument configuration.

15

Automation: Service Access and Channel Identification

Removable sensors and tool modules need connections that technicians can identify without tracing the whole machine. Combine permanent labels, connector keying and a clear circuit drawing. Route the lead so the release sleeve is accessible after guards and adjacent equipment are installed. Keep power and sensitive signal routes appropriate to the machine design. A service loop should provide useful access without rubbing against motion components or becoming a snag point.

16

Broadcast and Audio-Visual: Define the Channel

Camera power, control, timecode and audio leads may use compact circular connectors but carry entirely different circuits. Confirm the equipment pinout and direction before creating an extension or adapter. Coaxial channels require an appropriate impedance and termination, while power leads need a voltage-drop review at operating load. Mark both ends clearly. A connector brand alone does not establish compatibility with a particular camera or recording system.

17

Diagnostic Devices: Repeatable Connections

A removable diagnostic accessory needs repeatable electrical and mechanical behavior between visits or test sessions. Document the reference pinout, label, cable length and connector orientation so that a replacement is not only visually similar. Consider repeated wiping, storage bends and operator grip. Evaluation of an accessory should include the equipment-defined checks; a continuity pass alone does not establish the accuracy or safety of a diagnostic system.

18

Aerospace Projects: Specify Evidence Before Supply

A project-specific aerospace lead may require controlled materials, traceability, environmental tests and document retention beyond a general industrial assembly. Confirm those requirements before selecting parts or quoting. A familiar connector family is not proof that a cable is approved for flight or a defense program. The released drawing, accepted sources, manufacturing records and customer qualification route must describe the same configuration throughout the project.

19

Bend Protection: A Gradual Transition

The exit from a rigid connector to a flexible cable concentrates bending. A bend relief should spread curvature over a useful length rather than move the sharp bend to its own end. Review the cable jacket, relief stiffness and available installation space together. An overly rigid boot can be as troublesome as no support. Prototype evaluation should reproduce the actual angle and repeated motion, with the connector held as it will be in use.

20

Overmolding: Material, Adhesion and Serviceability

An overmold can provide shape control, protection and labeling at a cable junction. It does not automatically bond to every jacket or seal every hidden path. Match molding material and process conditions to the cable and insert, keeping the release mechanism free. Consider whether repair is required: a sealed-looking one-piece exterior can make internal rework impractical. Agree tooling, surface expectations and verification on a representative sample before production.

21

Cable Collets and Pull-Load Transfer

The cable collet must fit the actual jacket diameter, not just the conductor gauge. Its purpose is to transfer mechanical load through the supported jacket rather than the electrical termination. Cable ovality, soft insulation and dimensional tolerance affect the fit. Define an agreed pull check where required and inspect for both slip and jacket damage. Do not compensate for a wrong collet by tightening without reference to the assembly instructions.

22

High-Flex Duty: Observe the Complete Motion

Repeated bending, torsion and coil extension place different demands on conductors and insulation. Define the motion profile, travel, acceleration, temperature and target evaluation before choosing cable construction. Place clamps so movement occurs in the intended flex zone. A successful connector pull test is not evidence of dynamic cable life. If a prototype fails, review the location and failure mode before simply adding a thicker jacket or longer boot.

23

From Mating Sample to Controlled Pinout

Start with the equipment receptacle reference and a clearly stated view of the contacts. Front mating views and rear wiring views can appear mirrored. Confirm circuit names, polarity, shield assignment and any unused cavities before building a sample. Shell markings may identify only part of the connector designation, so retain the full approved part number. An adapter between brands is a new defined circuit, not automatic electrical interchangeability.

24

Solder, Crimp and Shield Termination

Termination follows the selected contact and cable. Soldering requires controlled stripping and heat input without allowing an excessively stiff solder-wicked section to enter the flex zone. Crimping requires the specified contact, wire range and tooling. The shield needs its own defined termination rather than incidental contact with the shell. Keep conductor preparation, insulation clearance and final inspection consistent between the approved sample and repeat builds.

25

Extensions: Check the Whole Electrical Path

An extension adds conductor length and another pair of contacts. For power this changes voltage drop and heating; for data it changes the channel loss and discontinuities. Retain the intended twisted pairs, coaxial construction and shield path through the assembly. Validate the complete connection with the actual equipment or agreed electrical method. A high-speed connector attached to an unsuitable cable cannot restore the performance lost elsewhere in the channel.

26

Sample Approval and Repeat Production

A useful sample approval records more than whether the plug fits. Confirm pinout, length datums, cable exit, labeling, latch access and the required inspection results. Transfer accepted choices into the drawing and material list before repeat production. Continuity and visual inspection form the basic release checks; additional electrical, mechanical or environmental checks follow the agreed project requirements. Changes to the cable, contact, plating or supplier need review before substitution.

Engineering and Purchasing Questions

Are these original LEMO parts or compatible alternatives?

The quotation and approved material list must identify which is supplied. Original parts and alternatives are not presented as the same product, and this page does not imply authorization by LEMO.

Are all push-pull connectors waterproof?

No. Protected indoor and sealed outdoor configurations differ. Confirm the exact series, cable seal and mating condition, then define any required assembly test.

Can you make an extension or adapter?

Yes, subject to review of both interfaces, pinout, cable length and signal or power requirements. Physical mating alone is insufficient to define an adapter.

What is needed for a coiled cable quotation?

Specify relaxed and usable extended lengths, coil diameter, straight tails, conductor requirements, connectors and expected movement. Restoring force and mounting space also need review.

Can a photograph identify the full connector?

It can start the review, but the complete designation, insert, contact orientation and cable collet may need drawings or the mating sample. We do not infer a final pinout from appearance.

Does a connector rating certify the finished cable?

No. Complete-assembly performance depends on the cable, termination, mounting and application. Required evidence and testing must be agreed for the actual configuration.
